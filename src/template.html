What the words mean

Evapotranspiration (ET)
Water leaving the field as evaporation from soil plus water plants draw up and release through their leaves. ET0 is the reference amount a well-watered grass surface would use; each crop uses a share of it that changes through its season.
Unmet water demand
The share of the water a crop wanted that the soil could not supply. Furrow tracks a daily soil-water account for your field: rain goes in, crop water use and runoff come out.
Soil moisture anomaly
How wet or dry the soil was compared with what is normal for that place and month. A dry anomaly in July means drier than a usual July, not drier than a desert.
Heavy rain on bare soil
Furrow counts rain on days with 12.7 mm or more, the cut-off the RUSLE erosion method uses to decide which storms matter. True erosivity depends on how hard the rain falls within half an hour, which daily data cannot show, so treat this as a rough exposure measure, not an erosion estimate.
Growing degree days
A running total of useful warmth. Each crop needs a certain total to mature, so this tells you whether a crop fits between your last spring frost and first autumn frost.
NDVI
A greenness score satellites calculate from red and near-infrared light. Dense healthy crops score high, bare soil scores low. Choose the greenness layer on the map to see it.
Cover crop
A crop grown between cash crops to protect and feed the soil, not to sell. Cereal rye survives winter; legumes such as vetch and clover also add nitrogen.
Satellite versus ground data
Satellites see every field on Earth regularly but in coarse blocks and only the top few centimetres of soil. A rain gauge or soil probe is exact but covers one spot. The best picture uses both, which is why Furrow asks for your soil and your knowledge.

How it works, and what it can’t do

What it does

  • Downloads daily maximum and minimum temperature, precipitation, root-zone soil wetness, sunlight reaching the ground, wind and humidity for your point from NASA POWER, 2001 to 2025. The sunlight values come from NASA's CERES satellite instruments.
  • Works out frost dates, heat, heavy rain, dry spells and evaporative demand (FAO Penman-Monteith), and tests each for a trend (Theil-Sen slope, Mann-Kendall test).
  • Builds crop calendars from your site's own temperatures using growing degree days, so sowing and harvest dates move with your climate.
  • Replays every rotation through all 25 years with a daily soil-water account, then scores water, soil, storm exposure, market fit and resilience.
  • Groups the 25 seasons into kinds of year two ways, with k-means clustering and with a simple thirds rule, and reports how far they agree.
  • Reshuffles the 25 years 300 times (a bootstrap) to see how often each alternative still outscores your current rotation.
  • Reads two years of MODIS greenness (NDVI) for a 250 m square at your point and compares it with the modelled crop calendar, as a field-scale check on the model.
  • Reads a 90 m elevation model on a 5 by 5 grid around your point to find steep ground and frost-prone hollows, and reads SoilGrids for a soil texture suggestion.

What it can’t see

  • NASA POWER describes a grid cell about 50 km across. It gives your district's climate, not the frost pocket at the bottom of your field.
  • Soil texture comes from you or from a 250 m global map. A soil test for organic matter, pH and compaction will always beat a texture class.
  • Market fit uses generic 1 to 5 ratings, not your local prices, contracts or input costs. It is not an income forecast.
  • Soil health is a proxy built from living-root days, legume nitrogen, residue, crop diversity and bare-soil days. Furrow does not measure organic matter or soil life.
  • Storm exposure uses daily rain totals, not measured storm intensity.
  • Crop traits are generic values in the range given by FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 and extension guides, not your varieties. The table below shows every value used.
  • Trend tags are exploratory: 25 years is a short record.
  • Pests, diseases, weeds, herbicide carry-over, equipment and labour are not modelled.
  • Calendars assume a temperate, frost-defined season. Results in the tropics are rough.
  • The model has not been checked against field trials or by an agronomist.

Who decides

  • You do. Furrow shows patterns and possibilities; it does not give instructions.
  • Take any option that interests you to an agronomist or extension adviser, and try it on a strip before the whole field.
  • Every rotation lists what needs local review before acting.
All the crop numbers we used
Data: NASA POWER daily agroclimatology (T2M_MAX, T2M_MIN, PRECTOTCORR, GWETROOT, ALLSKY_SFC_SW_DWN, WS2M, RH2M). Field greenness: MODIS MOD13Q1 through the ORNL DAAC subset service. Elevation: Copernicus DEM (ESA) through Open-Meteo. Soil texture: SoilGrids (ISRIC). Map layers: NASA GIBS (MODIS, SMAP, GPM IMERG). Satellite base map: Esri World Imagery. Crop coefficients follow FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56; reference evapotranspiration uses the FAO Penman-Monteith equation, with Hargreaves as a fallback.
